# Heads Up: resolving meeting disruptions quietly

A 10-week capstone with Webex's design team. We designed a low-effort, anonymous way to tell someone their mic is hot or they're sharing the wrong screen, without derailing the meeting. Every usability tester and peer reviewer said they'd want it, and Webex's design team took it as something to consider in their next feature review.

<Eyebrow>Problem</Eyebrow>

## Meetings get derailed by small, awkward disruptions

Someone shares the wrong screen. A forgotten mic broadcasts background noise to everyone. These moments are usually accidental, always awkward, and they add up to **meeting fatigue**: people mute, turn their cameras off and mentally check out.

The catch: most participants are passive bystanders. They notice the problem but don't want to be the one who interrupts to fix it.

<Img src="webex-heads-up/frame-1546.png" alt="Two common disruptions: a presenter sharing the wrong screen and a participant with a noisy hot mic" caption="Two of the most common disruptions: the wrong screen share and the rogue hot mic." />

**How might we** help people resolve disruptions in real time, discreetly and with almost no effort, so meetings stay productive?

<Accordion title="Why it matters to Webex: the business problem">

Meeting fatigue costs companies productivity, engagement and customer relationships. For Webex, competing with Zoom and Teams, making meetings feel more productive is a direct differentiator.

<Img src="webex-heads-up/3-item-module.png" alt="Three statistics: $3–5 million in productivity lost each year to technology issues; $544 billion lost each year to employee disengagement; 26% of inefficient meetings weaken customer relationships." caption="Source: Webex (2023)" />

<Eyebrow>What I did</Eyebrow>

## Research first, then one small idea, then tested it hard

**1. Research.** I started with what already existed: Webex's internal research reports, a competitive review of video-meeting apps and academic work on meeting fatigue. I used that to write our interview plan, and the team interviewed **6 people aged 21–55** who use video meetings for work. I led the synthesis.

Three insights shaped everything:

- **People hate disruptions,** like hot mics, choppy connections and buggy screen shares.
- **But they don't want to disrupt the disruptor.** Most participants are passive and won't unmute to say something.
- **Non-verbal tools work.** Chat, reactions and raise-hand are low-effort, and interviewees preferred them to speaking up.

<Callout by="Interview participant">

"I don't want to worry about audio feedback if someone's forgotten to turn off their mic… interrupting the flow."

</Callout>

**2. Define.** I turned the interviews into empathy and affinity maps, and worked with our content designer, Jacob, on personas and scenarios. We plotted ideas on an effort/value matrix and dot-voted. The winner was my sketch of a small pop-up "radial" alert tool. We validated the direction with Webex's product leads before designing.

- **User goal:** resolve disruptions in a low-effort, discreet way.
- **Webex goal:** improve real-time engagement to raise meeting productivity.

**4. Test and iterate.** I turned design-review feedback into a prioritized list, then led an **A/B usability test with 8 participants**. Two changes came out of it:

- **Anonymity matters.** Sending alerts under your name, and seeing an alert counter, made people anxious. We removed both.
- **Fewer choices.** The alert and participant screens overwhelmed people. We grouped alerts and filtered the participant list based on the alert chosen.

<Eyebrow>What I learned</Eyebrow>

## Small ideas can deliver the most value

A broad problem like meeting fatigue invites big ideas. Heads Up wasn't the flashiest option, but it solved both the user's problem and Webex's with the most value and feasibility.

## Keep poking holes

Even once the idea felt settled, our biggest improvements, anonymity and fewer choices, came from putting it in front of real users again.
