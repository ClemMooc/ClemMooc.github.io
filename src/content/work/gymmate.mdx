<Eyebrow>Why I built it</Eyebrow>

## The best gym app is the one you barely touch

<Grid cols={2}>

<div>

<Img src="gymmate/three-tools.png" alt="Diagram: three separate tools, a spreadsheet log, a warmup calculator, and a rest timer, with arrows merging into one GymMate app." caption="Three tools, one app." />

</div>

<div>

**Getting stronger runs on numbers, and mine were scattered across three apps I juggled between every set.**

I've trained on a 5x5 program for years: five sets of five reps at one weight, adding a little each session. That's progressive overload, and it only works if you have three things during a 90-second rest: last session's numbers, a warmup ladder for today's weight, and a timer.

That took three tools. A Google Sheets log I rebuilt by hand every day, a warmup calculator in Chrome, and a rest timer on my phone or Apple Watch.

**I spent my rest switching apps instead of resting. GymMate puts everything on one screen.**

</div>

</Grid>

<Eyebrow>How I built it</Eyebrow>

## Spec it, build it, test it at the gym

### A careful plan

I built GymMate with Lovable, an AI app builder, mainly because it includes a built-in backend (Supabase) for user accounts and saved data. With a builder like this, the first instructions shape everything that follows, so I wrote a complete spec before any code existed. Step one was a design plan only. No code until the plan held up.

It's like building a house. A basement is easy to include in the plans and hard to add later.

<Accordion title="What went into the spec">

I wrote the spec with a structured template I call my prompt wizard, then had other AI tools review it for gaps.

- Title and purpose. What the app is for and who it serves. Everything else follows from this.
- Look and feel, starting from a Figma Make mockup.
- The screens, tabs, buttons, and fields it needs.
- User flows through a whole workout, including what each button does.
- Every input, and what happens to it.
- A small set of sample data, 20 to 50 records, so the prototype stays quick.

</Accordion>

### Tested in real workouts

I used every version at the gym, and ran usability tests with two friends who lift. Problems show up quickly when you're tired and resting for 90 seconds. Each session ended with a short list of fixes for the next version.

<Eyebrow>What it does</Eyebrow>

## Three tabs: tracker, warmup, routines

<Grid cols={2} reveal="right">

<div>

### Tracker

It remembers your last session. Add an exercise, and last time's weight, sets, and reps are already there.

Tick off each set as you go, and note how hard it felt on a 1–10 scale (RPE). Drag exercises to reorder them. Bodyweight moves like planks skip the weight field.

It saves as you type. Your screen locks many times during a workout, so every entry is kept the moment you make it. It works offline too, and syncs when you reconnect.

</div>

<div>

### Around the tracker

The rest timer starts on its own when you tick off a set. Two minutes by default, and you can change it.

The calendar marks every day you trained. Tap one to see that workout.

Each exercise keeps its history: weight, reps, RPE, and your notes, for every session.

Export to Google Sheets or Excel, organized by week and day, in the layout of my original log.

</div>

</Grid>

<Grid cols={2} reveal="right">

<div>

### Warmup

Pick a lift, enter your working weight, and the warmup is ready. Or tap Warmup on any exercise in the tracker.

Warmup calculators cover many programs, each with its own formula. I only use 5x5, so I built in that one ladder:

1. Two sets of 5 with the empty bar
2. One set of 5 at about half your working weight
3. One set of 3 at about 70%
4. One set of 2 at about 90%
5. Then your five working sets

Every step rounds to plates you can load, listed per side. Set your own bar weights in settings, for a Smith machine, EZ bar, or trap bar.

</div>

### Routines

Save a routine once and load it in a tap. No more copying yesterday's rows into today.

Start from a preset, like Leg, Chest, or Bodyweight, or build your own program. Search the exercise list, or add a new one.

<Eyebrow>What I learned</Eyebrow>

### Plan before you build

Time spent on the spec saved time every week after. Fixes stayed small.

### Use it where it's used

The gym found problems a desk review never would.

### Change a little at a time

Small, specific fixes worked. Large batches of changes broke things.
